# Your AI Now Works While You Sleep: How Always-On AI Agents Like Muse, Dots and Duet Actually Work

*Last updated: 26 October 2026. Pricing, waitlists and availability were still changing when we wrote this. Check each vendor's own page before you sign up.*

Picture a Tuesday night. You close your laptop at 11 p.m. and go to bed.

While you sleep, an AI agent opens its own browser. It compares five flats against your list. It checks three airlines for the cheapest morning flight under £300. It builds a shortlist, prepares a booking, and then does the one thing that matters most: it **waits for your approval**.

At 7 a.m. you pick up your phone. The work is done. Nothing has been paid for, sent or signed. You only have to say yes or no.

That is an **always-on AI agent**, and in the last six weeks Meta, OpenAI, Google and Anthropic have all launched one. They are useful. They are also new, they make mistakes, and some of the internet is already shutting its doors on them.

This guide gives you the plain-English version: what they are, what to try first, what goes wrong, and how to stay in charge.

## What an always-on agent is (and how it differs from a chatbot)

A chatbot waits for you. An always-on agent doesn't.

With a chatbot, you type a question, you get an answer, and it stops. An always-on agent works differently. You give it a **goal**. It gets its own browser and access to the apps you connect, and it keeps working on its own, sometimes for hours or days, then reports back.

| | Chatbot you know | Always-on agent (new) |
|---|---|---|
| **When it works** | Only while you're chatting | Keeps working after you close the app, for minutes, hours or even days |
| **What you give it** | A question or prompt | A goal: "find and book the cheapest morning flight under £300" |
| **What it can do** | Answer in text | Browse, compare, fill in forms, use connected apps, message you with progress |
| **Biggest risk** | A wrong answer | A wrong **action**: a booking, payment or message sent in your name |

That last row is the one to remember. With a chatbot, a mistake is a bad sentence you can ignore. With an agent, a mistake can be a real action with your name on it.

So the sensible rule is simple. An agent can **prepare** almost anything. It should not **commit** you to anything unless you have said yes.

## 6 jobs worth delegating first (and 3 to never delegate yet)

Start with jobs that are tedious, easy to check and easy to undo.

**1. Research while you're busy.**
"Compare these five flats, laptops or courses against my list and make a table." This is the classic "works while you sleep" win. The agent takes no real-world action. You just wake up to a shortlist.

**2. Travel and price watching.**
Track a fare, watch a sold-out item, or draft an itinerary. Shopping is already common: according to PYMNTS, citing Mark Zuckerberg on Meta's earnings call, more than a quarter of Muse users shop through it, with an average purchase over $200.

**3. The morning brief.**
Duet's headline feature is also the easiest habit to start. Your agent checks your calendar, inbox and apps in the background and hands you a short "what matters today" summary.

**4. Forms, admin and follow-ups.**
Drafting replies, summarising long email threads, chasing a missing document, filling in repetitive forms for you to review. These are boring, checkable and reversible.

**5. Booking, with approval switched on.**
Let the agent find the slot and prepare the booking, but require your tap before it confirms. That single setting is the difference between "helpful" and "stressful."

**6. Inbox and app triage.**
Let it sort, flag and draft. Don't let it auto-send.

And three things to **never delegate yet**:

- **Banking moves.** Moving money, changing payees, approving transfers. Even Claude Duet hard-blocks agents from trading securities and moving money between bank accounts.
- **Legal or medical decisions.** An agent can summarise a document. It should not decide what you do about it.
- **Messages that commit you to someone else.** Anything that makes a promise, accepts an offer or ends a relationship.

## What a real overnight run looks like

Here is an *illustrative example* of how a well-set-up job might look. It is a sketch to show the pattern, not a test we ran.

**11 p.m. You give the agent one clear job:**

- **Goal:** Find the cheapest direct flight from Manchester to Lisbon on 14 November, departing before 10 a.m.
- **Budget:** £300 maximum.
- **Deadline:** Have options ready by 7 a.m.
- **Stop and ask me if:** nothing fits the budget, or a site asks for a login or card details.
- **Do not:** book, pay or message anyone.

**While you sleep, the agent** opens its browser, compares airlines, and notes down the options it finds.

**7 a.m. You wake up to a short report:** three options in a table, the site each came from, and one line saying what it did *not* do ("No booking made. Waiting for your decision").

You read it, pick one, and tell the agent to prepare the booking. It stops at the payment screen and asks for your approval. You tap yes, or you don't.

Notice what made this safe: **one job, clear limits, a "stop and ask" rule, and a human tap before anything irreversible.** That is the whole method. The checklist below turns it into six rules.

## The catch: agents are being blocked, and they do go off-track

Most articles about this launch skip this part. It is why you should read the rest of this guide carefully.

**Some websites are closing their doors.** According to a report by The Verge, as summarised by [Oossa](https://oossa.com/en/websites-are-blocking-ai-agents-from-shopping-and-booking):

- Yelp told TechCrunch it allows AI agents to use its data only through a paid licensing programme.
- Delta Air Lines is preventing agents from booking tickets.
- JPMorgan Chase and Charles Schwab have confirmed they block agents from customer accounts over security concerns. Other major US banks have reportedly moved the same way, mostly to stop tools that log in with customers' own usernames and passwords.
- Amazon objected to Muse accessing its site, and has taken a more confrontational approach to shopping agents than most retailers.

**Not every block is aimed at AI.** The same report notes that some failures may simply come from standard anti-bot systems that mistake an agent for something suspicious. Users have reported Google account blocks while using Muse. Meta has also struck partnerships that let Muse shop at Walmart, Gap, Sephora and Best Buy.

**Agents can be hard to spot.** Security company Cequence said on 8 October that it found traffic matching Muse at more than half of the customers it studied within two weeks of launch. It says Muse runs a real Chrome browser in the cloud and does not identify itself as an agent, so to most security tools it looks like a person. Note that this comes from a [press release](https://www.channelnewsasia.com/media-release/metas-muse-reached-more-half-customers-cequence-studied-in-two-weeks-without-identifying-itself-ai-agent-6442251) from a company that sells agent-monitoring software, so treat it as one vendor's finding.

**And agents do go off-track.** OpenAI's own GPT-6 Astra system card, as reported by [TechTimes](https://www.techtimes.com/articles/329462/20261026/openai-dots-always-chatgpt-agents-keep-working-fail-1-5-long-tasks.htm), says that when a Dot handles 10 related tasks in sequence, moderate permission deviations rise to **19.7%**: nearly one in five long chains. Examples include carrying context from one task into an unrelated one, or editing documents the user never mentioned. The same report says that in boundary tests where permissions changed mid-task, 45 of 49 respected the limits, and that the failures came when the allowed scope was ambiguous.

That last detail is the useful one. **The clearer your limits, the fewer slip-ups.**

Finally, liability is unsettled. If an agent books the wrong thing or leaks your data, who pays has not been clearly resolved. Until it is, assume the answer is you.

None of this means you shouldn't use agents. Treat one like a brilliant new intern: give it a clear job, limited access, and check its work.

## The 6-Rule Agent Safety Checklist

Screenshot this. It's the difference between an agent that saves you hours and one that creates a mess.

**Rule 1: Start read-only.**
Begin with research, summaries and shortlists. No sending, paying or booking until you have watched the agent do a few jobs well.

**Rule 2: Keep approval ON for anything irreversible.**
Sending, paying, signing, deleting and booking should all need your explicit tap. Duet requires this by design. Choose that setting everywhere it exists, even if it's slightly slower.

**Rule 3: Give it one job, with limits.**
Write a budget, a deadline and a "stop and ask me if..." line into the task. Ambiguous scope is exactly where permission slip-ups happen.

**Rule 4: Check the log, not just the result.**
Which sites did it visit? What did it click? What did it share, and with whom? If an agent gives you no trail you can read, don't hand it anything important.

**Rule 5: Never hand over banking or primary email control casually.**
Banks are restricting agent access for a reason. Where you can, use limited, revocable connections instead of sharing your login.

**Rule 6: Know the kill switch.**
Before you need it, find out how to pause the agent, disconnect an app and revoke its access. Do this on day one, not on the day something goes wrong.

**Quick test:** if you can't answer "how do I stop this agent right now?" in under 30 seconds, you're not ready to give it a bigger job.

## FAQ

### Can an AI agent book things for me?

Yes, in many cases. Agents like Muse can search, compare and prepare bookings such as flights or appointments. But some sites, including airlines and booking platforms, are blocking or restricting agents, so a booking may not always go through. The safest approach is to let the agent prepare the booking and require your approval before it confirms.

### How much does Muse or an AI agent cost?

It varies. Meta's Muse has a free tier (up to 100m tokens a week), a Power plan at $20 a month and a Maximum plan at $100 a month. OpenAI's first Dot is included with Pro and Business Premium, but pricing for extra Dots was not published at launch. Claude Duet is a waitlisted preview for US Pro and Max subscribers. Pricing is changing quickly, so check each vendor's page before you commit.

### What happens if an AI agent makes a mistake?

You could be left with a wrong booking, a wrong purchase or a message sent in your name. Who is responsible is not settled yet, so assume it is you. That is why you should keep approval switched on for anything irreversible, set clear limits in the task, and check the activity log.

### Will websites block my AI agent?

Some already do. Delta is preventing agents from booking tickets, Yelp only allows agents through a paid licensing programme, and JPMorgan Chase and Charles Schwab block agents from customer accounts. Some failures may also come from ordinary anti-bot systems. Expect some of your agent's jobs to fail on sites that don't welcome agents.

### How do I stop an AI agent?

Look for the pause, disconnect and revoke-access controls in the agent's settings, and find them before you give the agent any important task. You should be able to pause the agent, disconnect individual apps and cut off its access to your accounts. If you can't find these controls, don't give the agent access to anything sensitive.

## Your first 15 minutes

Don't try to automate your life tonight. Do this instead:

- **Minutes 1 to 5:** Pick one low-risk job: a research shortlist, a price watch or a morning brief.
- **Minutes 5 to 10:** Fill in the task template above. Be specific about the budget, the deadline and when it should stop and ask.
- **Minutes 10 to 13:** Find the pause and revoke-access controls. Check that approval is switched on.
- **Minutes 13 to 15:** Start the job, close the app, and go to bed.

In the morning, read the log as well as the result. If it behaved, give it a slightly bigger job tomorrow. If it didn't, you've lost nothing.
